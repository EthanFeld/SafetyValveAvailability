An NFL tracking study

The hidden value of a safety valve.

When the pocket breaks down, an easy throw can give the quarterback a way out. A receiver can create that option without getting a target. Safety Valve Availability (SVA) uses tracking data to put those overlooked routes on the record.

01 The missing credit

A route can matter without a target.

Targets, catches and yards tell you where the ball went. They leave out the back who slipped into the flat or the tight end who gave the quarterback a short throw before the rush arrived.

Against Denver, James Robinson cleared the model’s opening threshold on every active pressure frame of this third down. Trevor Lawrence took a four-yard sack. The box score records the sack. The film also shows the outlet Jacksonville had available.

02 How SVA works

Measure the throw the quarterback has.

SVA follows the routes in reach while the quarterback is under pressure. It grades the quality of each opportunity and compares receivers with similar chances.

  1. 01

    Find the window

    Start when a credited rusher closes in. Follow the play until the pass, sack or run. A route is eligible only when the receiver projects within 20 yards of the quarterback, so a receiver running deeper stays outside the sample until they come into range.

  2. 02

    Read the throwing lane

    Score projected separation, catch radius, distance, bodies in the lane and the quarterback’s throwing hand. Those details distinguish a short, clear throw from one the quarterback has to squeeze through traffic.

  3. 03

    Grade comparable routes

    Estimate catch probability and EPA if targeted, then compare them with position and situation benchmarks. The EPA component accounts for catch probability so the grade can distinguish catchability from the value of the play.

03 What the test found

Timing adds detail to the film.

Looking beyond onset captured 265 more in-range routes. The later frames let you see who became an option after pressure arrived and how long the quarterback had that throw.

On held-out games, adding early opening timing modestly improved catch prediction in a matched sample. Team EPA prediction showed essentially the same error, and onset grades were more stable across split samples. Use onset to compare receivers and the full window to see when their openings appeared.

Forecasts learn from observed targets. For untargeted outlets, they estimate what a throw might yield. The eight-week sample measures available options and leaves the effect of choosing a different target open for further study.
